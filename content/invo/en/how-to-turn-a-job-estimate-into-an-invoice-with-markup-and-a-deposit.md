# How to turn a job estimate into an invoice with markup and a deposit

The estimate is the promise; the invoice is the bill. Most billing disputes on small jobs come from the gap between the two: a number changed, a deposit was forgotten, or the customer never saw how the total was built. The fix is to keep one set of line items from the first estimate to the last invoice, and to show markup, tax, and the deposit the same way on both.

## Estimate, quote, and invoice: what each one does

- **Estimate** — your best calculation of what the job will cost. It can change once you open the wall.
- **Quote** — a fixed price for a defined scope. If the customer accepts, that is the price.
- **Invoice** — the request for payment. It references the work done, what was already paid, and what is still due.

Using the same line items across all three means the customer can compare them line by line, and you never retype a number.

## Step 1: Build the estimate from line items

Break the job into lines with a quantity, a unit, and a unit cost. "Tile — 120 sq ft × $9.50" is easier to approve than "Bathroom tile: $1,140", and much easier to adjust when the scope changes.

Keep labor on its own lines (hours × rate), separate from materials. That makes markup and tax decisions clearer later.

## Step 2: Add your markup

Markup is the percentage you add on top of cost to cover overhead and profit. Add it either per line or once on the subtotal — but do it the same way on the estimate and the invoice so the totals match.

If you are unsure whether you are pricing on markup or margin, read our guide on [markup vs margin](/invo/blog/markup-vs-margin-for-contractors) before you send the number.

## Step 3: Apply sales tax

Tax rules depend on where you work: some places tax materials only, some tax labor too. Put tax on its own line so the customer sees it, and use the same rate on the invoice that you showed on the estimate.

## Step 4: Take the deposit and record it

When the customer accepts, collect the deposit and record it against the document right away — date, amount, and method. That record is what keeps the final invoice honest. See [how much deposit to ask for](/invo/blog/how-much-deposit-should-a-contractor-ask-for) for typical amounts and legal caps.

## Step 5: Turn it into the invoice and bill the balance

When the work is done, issue the invoice from the same line items. Add any approved change orders as new lines, show the deposit already paid, and bill the balance due.

## Worked example: a bathroom remodel

| Line | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Materials | $4,200.00 |
| Labor — 40 h × $65 | $2,600.00 |
| Cost subtotal | $6,800.00 |
| Markup 20% | $1,360.00 |
| Subtotal before tax | $8,160.00 |
| Sales tax 8% | $652.80 |
| **Total** | **$8,812.80** |
| Deposit paid (30%) | −$2,643.84 |
| **Balance due** | **$6,168.96** |

The customer sees the same lines on the estimate and the invoice. The only new line on the invoice is the deposit they already paid.

## Checklist before you send the invoice

- Line items match the accepted estimate, plus signed change orders
- Markup and tax are applied the same way as on the estimate
- The deposit and any progress payments are recorded with dates
- Payment terms and due date are on the document
- Your business name, logo, and signature are on the PDF
